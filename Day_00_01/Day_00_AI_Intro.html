The one-line definition to write on the board: Artificial Intelligence is making a machine do something that, if a human did it, we'd say required intelligence: recognising, predicting, deciding, creating. Everything else in this course is "how".
Open with a question, not a definition. Ask: "Who used AI today?" Most will say "no one". Then ask who unlocked their phone with their face, used Google Maps, watched a YouTube recommendation, or typed a message with autocorrect. Every hand goes up. That gap between "I don't use AI" and "I used it 20 times before breakfast" is the hook for the whole hour.
Stop 02

Why is everyone talking about this now?

AI research is 70 years old. Three things finally lined up in the last decade.

7 min05 → 12
Ingredient 1

Data

Every photo, click, review, sensor reading and chat message became a training example. The internet accidentally built the world's largest textbook for machines. A modern language model reads more text than a human could in ten thousand lifetimes.

Ingredient 2

Compute

Graphics cards built for video games turned out to be perfect for the maths of learning: billions of tiny multiplications in parallel. A single GPU today does what a room-sized supercomputer did in 2000.

Ingredient 3

One big idea

In 2017 a paper called "Attention Is All You Need" introduced the Transformer. Nearly every headline model since (ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Llama) is a descendant of that one architecture. We build one from scratch in Week 5.

Why it matters for you: the people who understand the internals, not just the chat box, are the ones being hired. This course is about internals.
Frame this as "the fuel, the engine, and the blueprint". Data is fuel, GPUs are the engine, Transformers are the blueprint. Mention that all three were individually available for years; the explosion came from combining them at scale around 2012 (AlexNet) and 2017 (Transformer). Don't go deep into what a Transformer is yet, just plant the name.

Stop 04

The long road: from counting beads to thinking machines

AI didn't appear in 2022. It's the latest step in a 4,000-year project of making machines do our thinking for us. Each era solved one problem and exposed the next.

13 min20 → 33
~2500 BCE → 1800s · MECHANICAL

Machines that count

The abacus, then Pascal's adding machine (1642), then Babbage's Analytical Engine (1837), a steam-powered computer that was designed but never fully built. Ada Lovelace wrote the first "program" for it and famously noted the machine could only do "whatever we know how to order it to perform". Hold onto that sentence; the entire history of AI is the story of proving it wrong.

Babbage's engine ↗
1936 · THE IDEA OF A COMPUTER

Alan Turing's imaginary machine

Before any electronic computer existed, a 24-year-old Turing asked: what is the simplest machine that could compute anything computable? His answer was a tape, a read/write head, and a table of rules. That's it. Every computer, phone and GPU you've ever touched is a fast, fancy Turing machine. In 1950 he asked the follow-up: "Can machines think?", and proposed the Turing Test.

1945–1970 · THE COMPUTER + THE PROGRAM

ENIAC, then software

ENIAC (1945) weighed 27 tonnes and was "programmed" by physically rewiring it. Then came stored programs: instructions living in memory as data. Then languages: FORTRAN (1957), C (1972), Python (1991). A program is a list of exact rules a human wrote: if temperature > 30: turn on the fan. The computer never guesses. This is the world Ada described.

ENIAC footage ↗
1956–2000 · "AI" IS BORN, THEN STALLS

Dartmouth, expert systems, two winters

The term "Artificial Intelligence" was coined at a 1956 workshop at Dartmouth. Early optimism was wild ("solved in a summer"). The dominant approach for 40 years was symbolic AI: encode human knowledge as thousands of hand-written rules. It beat Kasparov at chess (Deep Blue, 1997) but couldn't tell a cat from a dog, because nobody can write down the rules for "cat". Funding collapsed twice: the AI winters.

The 1956 proposal ↗
2012 → TODAY · LEARNING REPLACES RULES

The deep learning explosion

In 2012 a neural network called AlexNet, trained on 1.2 million labelled photos using two gaming GPUs, crushed every hand-written vision system at once. The trick was ancient (neural nets are from the 1950s); the data and compute were new. From there: speech recognition (2014), AlphaGo beating the world Go champion (2016), the Transformer (2017), GPT-3 (2020), ChatGPT (Nov 2022, 100 million users in 2 months), and now models that reason, see, hear and act.

AlphaGo documentary ↗
The single most important sentence in this section is Lovelace's: "only whatever we know how to order it to perform". Read it aloud twice. Then say: the moment a machine did something nobody ordered it to do (AlexNet learning what a cat looks like from photos), the definition of programming changed. That's machine learning. If time is short, skip the Dartmouth era and go straight from ENIAC to 2012.
Stop 05

The ladder: each rung hands the machine more of the thinking

Read left to right. At every step, the human writes less and the machine decides more. Notice where "rules" stop being written by people.

7 min33 → 40
RUNG 1

Machine

Turing's tape. Does exactly one fixed thing. Human does 100% of the thinking.

RUNG 2

Program

A list of exact instructions. if x > 30. Human writes every rule.

RUNG 3

Software

Thousands of programs working together: Excel, WhatsApp, a bank. Still all rules, just many.

RUNG 4

Automation

Software that runs without a human clicking: cron jobs, factory robots, auto-emails. Rules on a schedule.

RUNG 5 · THE JUMP

Machine Learning

Human gives examples, not rules. The machine writes the rules itself. "Here are 10,000 spam emails, figure out spam."

RUNG 6

Deep Learning

ML with big neural networks. Learns from raw pixels, audio, text. No human features. AlexNet, YOLO, speech-to-text.

RUNG 7

Generative AI

Deep learning that creates: text, images, code, video. ChatGPT, Midjourney. Trained on the whole internet.

RUNG 8

Agentic AI

A GenAI model in a loop with tools: it plans, acts (search, run code, call APIs), observes, and retries until the goal is met.

RUNG 9 · WHERE WE'RE HEADED

AI software developer

An agent that takes a ticket, reads your codebase, writes code, runs tests, fixes failures, opens the PR. Devin, Claude Code, Cursor agents. The tool and the co-worker.

YOU

The builder

Someone has to design, train, evaluate, deploy and supervise all of this. By Day 45, that's you.

Watch the pattern: Rungs 1–4 are "human writes rules, machine executes". Rungs 5–9 are "human gives examples and goals, machine figures out rules and actions". That line, between rung 4 and 5, is the most important line in this course.
Physically walk across the room as you go up the ladder; stop at the gap between 4 and 5 and stand there. Ask: "In a spam filter, who wrote the rule 'emails containing FREE MONEY are spam'?" Answer: nobody. The model found it. That's the jump. For rung 9, show a 60-second clip of Claude Code or Cursor agent mode fixing a bug if you have one saved; students who've only seen chatbots find it startling.
Stop 06

AI ⊃ ML ⊃ DL ⊃ GenAI. And agents sit on top.

People use these words interchangeably. They are not the same thing. They nest like Russian dolls: each inner ring is a smaller, more specific subset of the outer one.

6 min40 → 46
Artificial IntelligenceThe umbrella term. Chess engines with hand-written rules, a thermostat "learning" your schedule, ChatGPT: all AI. Some of it is not machine learning at all.
Machine LearningA subset of AI where the rules come from data, not programmers. Predict house prices, detect fraud, group customers. Weeks 1–2 of this course. Mostly maths you already half-know: lines, distances, probabilities.
Deep LearningML using neural networks with many layers. Handles messy raw data: images, sound, language. Needs lots of data and GPUs. Weeks 3–5: we build a neural network from scratch in NumPy before touching PyTorch.
Generative AIDeep learning models trained to produce new things: LLMs write text and code, diffusion models paint images. Week 6: LLMs, fine-tuning, RAG.
Agentic AINot a new kind of model. It's a GenAI model wrapped in a loop: think → act with a tool → observe → repeat. Week 7 and the capstones: LangGraph, CrewAI, multi-agent systems.

This is the answer to "how does Quick, Draw! know it's a cat?"

Classical programming

Rules+Data→Answers

Human writes if has_whiskers and has_tail: cat. Computer applies it. Breaks the moment a cat hides its tail.

⟶

Machine learning

Data+Answers→Rules

Human shows 10,000 photos labelled "cat" / "not cat". Computer finds the rules itself. The rules it finds are millions of numbers no human ever wrote or can fully read.

Click each definition on the left to highlight its ring. Then draw the Rules+Data→Answers vs Data+Answers→Rules diagram on the whiteboard yourself even though it's on screen; students remember what they watch being drawn. Common confusion to pre-empt: "Is ChatGPT AI or ML or DL?" Answer: all of them at once, because the rings nest. It's an AI system, built with ML, specifically DL, specifically a generative model.
Stop 07 · Hands-on

Watch a machine learn. Right now. In your browser.

Click anywhere on the canvas to drop data points. Then press Learn. The red line starts random and wrong. Each step, it measures how wrong it is and nudges itself a little less wrong. That's it. That is the entire secret of machine learning: gradient descent. We derive it properly on Day 3.

8 min46 → 54
points: 0
steps: 0
error (MSE): –
line: y = ? x + ?

What you're actually watching

Guess. The model starts with a random line: y = w·x + b. Two numbers, w and b. These are its "parameters". GPT-4 has about a trillion of them; the idea is identical.
Measure the mistake. For every point, how far is the line from it? Square that, average it. One number: the loss. Big loss = bad line.
Nudge. Calculus tells us which direction to move w and b to make the loss slightly smaller. Move a tiny bit that way.
Repeat a few hundred times. The line settles onto the data. Nobody told it the answer. It found the rule from the examples.
Every model in this course is this loop, scaled up. A neural network is the same thing with millions of w's stacked in layers. ChatGPT is the same loop, where "the line" is "the next word" and "the points" are the internet.

Want the full version with real Python? Google Colab gives you a free GPU in the browser; that's our lab for all 45 days. Bookmark it today.

Preview of Week 3 · what a neural network looks like

Left: inputs (pixels, words, numbers). Middle: layers of tiny "neurons", each doing a weighted sum + a squash. Right: the answer. Every line is one learnable number. Watch the pulses: that's data flowing forward. Learning flows backward (Day 12: backpropagation).

Let a student drive. Drop points in a rough line, press Learn, and narrate the error number dropping. Then drop one wild outlier and watch the line get pulled: that's a 30-second introduction to why data quality matters. Use "One step" to slow it down and show that each step is tiny. If someone asks "what's the maths?", write loss = mean((wx+b−y)²) on the board and say "Day 3". Don't go further today.

Quick check

AI, ML, or just a program?

A calculator app that adds two numbers.

Just a program. Every rule was written by a human. Fast, correct, and not intelligent.

Gmail deciding an email is spam.

Machine learning. Nobody wrote "emails with 'lottery' are spam"; the model learned it from millions of labelled examples.

Asking ChatGPT to write a poem about Hyderabad rain.

Generative AI (which is also ML, which is also AI). It produces something new that wasn't in any training example.

A thermostat: if temp > 25°C, switch on the AC.

A program. One hand-written rule. Marketing might call it "smart", but there's no learning.

Netflix putting a show on your homepage.

ML. A recommendation model predicts what you'll watch from what people like you watched.

An agent that reads a bug report, edits your code, and opens a pull request.

Agentic AI: a generative model (LLM) in a loop with tools. The top rung of the ladder, and Week 7.
