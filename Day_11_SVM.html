01 · Hook

Where would you paint the divider?

Two lanes of traffic on the Mumbai–Pune Expressway. You must paint one line between them. Any line that keeps the two lanes apart "works". But an engineer paints it as far as possible from both lanes, so a small swerve from either side does not cross it. That extra room is the margin, and the SVM is the algorithm that maximises it.

Ask the class

Why not just use logistic regression?

Logistic regression is happy with any line that gets the probabilities roughly right, and every training point pulls on it a little. The SVM ignores the well-classified crowd entirely: only the points on the edge of the street, the support vectors, have a say. Ask: "if I delete a point far from the boundary, does the SVM change?" No. "If I delete a support vector?" Yes. That is a property no other model we have seen has.

Why this matters in 2026

SVMs lost the image and speech wars to deep learning after 2012. They did not lose tables with fewer than ~50k rows, high-dimensional sparse text, and anything where you have an embedding and need a fast, robust classifier on top. LinearSVC on TF-IDF or on sentence embeddings is still the fastest strong baseline in NLP, and "explain the kernel trick" is still one of the top five classical interview questions.

02 · Concept

Margin, support vectors, and the trick

Three ideas. Maximise the street width (margin). Let a few points cross it, at a price (soft margin, controlled by C). Measure similarity instead of position so the street can curve (kernels, controlled by gamma).

18min
🪙

Coins on a table

Scatter ten-rupee coins in a ring around a pile of one-rupee coins on a table. No ruler laid flat can separate the two. Now bang the table: the light one-rupee coins in the middle jump higher than the heavy ten-rupee coins on the outside. For that instant, a flat sheet of paper held at the right height separates them perfectly. Seen from above, the sheet's edge is a circle. The SVM never builds the 3D table-bang; the kernel just tells it how similar two coins would be up there. That is the trick: compute similarities in a higher dimension without ever going there.

Live: margin, support vectors, C and gamma

The canvas trains a real SVM in your browser (Platt's SMO algorithm, the same idea inside LIBSVM). The decision boundary is the solid line; the two dashed lines are the margin edges where f(x) = ±1; ringed points are the support vectors, the only points that determine the boundary. Change the dataset, then drag C and gamma and watch the street widen, narrow, bend, and eventually wrap every single point (overfitting). Click on the canvas to add points of the selected class and see whether the boundary moves.

Key idea 1 · Maximum margin

Of all separating lines, pick the one whose distance to the nearest point of either class is largest. Wider street = a boundary that tolerates noise = better generalisation. Only the nearest points (support vectors) matter; delete the rest and nothing changes.

Key idea 2 · C is the price of a violation

Real data overlaps, so we allow points inside the street or on the wrong side, but charge C per unit of violation. Large C: violations are expensive, the street narrows to avoid them, more risk of overfitting. Small C: violations are cheap, the street widens, more points become support vectors, smoother boundary. C is the SVM's regularisation knob (inverse of the usual λ).

Key idea 3 · gamma is the reach of one point

With the RBF kernel K(a,b) = exp(−γ‖a−b‖²), gamma sets how far a training point's influence extends. Small gamma: every point influences a wide area, boundary is smooth, almost linear. Large gamma: influence dies within a tiny radius, the boundary wraps individual points, overfitting. sklearn's default gamma='scale' = 1 / (n_features · Var(X)).

Interview answer

"An SVM finds the hyperplane that maximises the margin, the distance to the nearest training points, which are called support vectors. The soft-margin parameter C trades margin width against misclassification. The kernel trick replaces the dot product with a kernel function, which is equivalent to mapping the data to a higher-dimensional space where a linear separator exists, without computing that mapping explicitly. RBF kernels with gamma control how local the boundary is."

03 · Deeper

The maths, kept honest

Four formulas. Each is decoded in one sentence, then we look at the loss function that makes an SVM an SVM.

10min

1. The decision function and the margin

f(x) = w·x + b,   predict sign(f(x)),   margin width = 2 / ‖w‖

w is the normal vector to the boundary, b the offset. The street edges are f(x) = +1 and f(x) = −1; the distance between them is 2/‖w‖. So "maximise the margin" is the same as "minimise ‖w‖", which is why the optimisation below has ½‖w‖² in it.

2. The soft-margin objective (what sklearn actually solves)

minw,b  ½‖w‖² + C Σi max(0, 1 − yi(w·xi + b))

The first term wants a wide street. The second is the hinge loss summed over points: zero when a point is on the correct side and outside the street (y·f ≥ 1), growing linearly as it enters the street or crosses the line. C is the exchange rate between the two wishes. Labels yi are ±1, not 0/1, so that y·f is positive exactly when the prediction is right.

3. The dual, where kernels live

f(x) = Σi αi yi K(xi, x) + b,   0 ≤ αi ≤ C

Solving the Lagrangian dual gives one weight αi per training point instead of one per feature. Most αi are exactly zero; the non-zero ones are the support vectors. Crucially, the data appears only inside K(xi, x), a similarity between two points. Swap the dot product for any valid kernel and you get a non-linear classifier for free. That is the whole kernel trick in one line.

Cost: training is O(n²) to O(n³) in the number of rows because the kernel matrix is n×n. Past ~50–100k rows, use LinearSVC (liblinear, linear in n), SGDClassifier(loss='hinge'), or approximate the kernel with RBFSampler / Nystroem.

4. Practical rules that save you an afternoon

Always scale features

The RBF kernel uses Euclidean distance; a column in rupees will dominate a column in years. On Telco churn, unscaled RBF-SVC scores 73.5% accuracy; with StandardScaler in a Pipeline it scores 79.3%. Trees did not care; SVMs and KNN do.

Search C and gamma on a log grid

C ∈ {0.01 … 1000}, gamma ∈ {0.001 … 10}, powers of 10 first, then refine. They interact: a larger gamma often wants a smaller C. Use GridSearchCV with 5-fold stratified CV (formal treatment on Day 13).

Probabilities are not native

SVC outputs a signed distance, not a probability. probability=True fits a separate Platt-scaling logistic regression via internal 5-fold CV: slow and slightly inconsistent with predict. For ROC-AUC use decision_function instead; it ranks exactly the same.

How to say this in an interview

"The SVM minimises ½‖w‖² plus C times the hinge loss, which is zero for points outside the margin, so only support vectors shape the boundary. In the dual, the data appears only through inner products, so replacing them with a kernel like RBF gives a non-linear boundary at the cost of O(n²) training. In practice I scale features, grid-search C and gamma on a log scale, and switch to LinearSVC for large or sparse data such as TF-IDF."

SVC on shapes, LinearSVC on text

Snippet 1 · Three kernels on two shapes

python
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons, make_circles
from sklearn.svm import SVC
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

shapes = {"moons":   make_moons(n_samples=400, noise=0.25, random_state=42),
          "circles": make_circles(n_samples=400, noise=0.10, factor=0.4, random_state=42)}

for name, (X, y) in shapes.items():
    X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.3, random_state=42)
    for kernel in ["linear", "poly", "rbf"]:
        clf = SVC(kernel=kernel, C=1.0, gamma="scale", degree=3).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
        print(f"{name:8s} {kernel:7s} test acc={clf.score(X_te, y_te):.3f}  support vectors={clf.n_support_.sum()}")
# moons    linear  0.817 (102 SV)   poly 0.858 (117)   rbf 0.908 (83)
# circles  linear  0.542            poly 0.650         rbf 1.000   ← a line cannot cut a ring

Snippet 2 · Draw the street and the support vectors

python
import numpy as np, matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from sklearn.inspection import DecisionBoundaryDisplay

X, y = shapes["moons"]
clf = SVC(kernel="rbf", C=1, gamma=1).fit(X, y)

disp = DecisionBoundaryDisplay.from_estimator(clf, X, response_method="decision_function",
                                              plot_method="contour", levels=[-1, 0, 1],
                                              linestyles=["--", "-", "--"], colors="k")   # margin edges + boundary
disp.ax_.scatter(X[:, 0], X[:, 1], c=y, cmap="coolwarm", s=18)
disp.ax_.scatter(clf.support_vectors_[:, 0], clf.support_vectors_[:, 1],       # ring the support vectors
                 s=90, facecolors="none", edgecolors="k")
plt.title(f"RBF SVM: {len(clf.support_vectors_)} support vectors of {len(X)} points"); plt.show()

Snippet 3 · Tune C and gamma properly (scale first, grid on a log scale)

python
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV, StratifiedKFold

pipe = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SVC(kernel="rbf"))       # scaler INSIDE the pipeline → no leakage in CV
grid = {"svc__C": [0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100],
        "svc__gamma": [0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10]}                  # 25 combos × 5 folds = 125 fits, seconds on moons
gs = GridSearchCV(pipe, grid, cv=StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=42),
                  scoring="accuracy", n_jobs=-1).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
print(gs.best_params_, round(gs.best_score_, 3), "→ test", round(gs.score(X_te, y_te), 3))

# heat-map of the 5×5 grid (see notebook): the best region is a diagonal band, large gamma ↔ small C

Snippet 4 · Text classification: TF-IDF + LinearSVC (still the fastest strong baseline)

python
from sklearn.datasets import fetch_20newsgroups
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer
from sklearn.svm import LinearSVC

cats = ["rec.sport.hockey", "sci.space", "comp.graphics", "talk.politics.misc"]
train = fetch_20newsgroups(subset="train", categories=cats, remove=("headers", "footers", "quotes"))
test  = fetch_20newsgroups(subset="test",  categories=cats, remove=("headers", "footers", "quotes"))

vec = TfidfVectorizer(sublinear_tf=True, min_df=2, ngram_range=(1, 2))   # 2,242 docs → 59,110 sparse features
X_tr = vec.fit_transform(train.data); X_te = vec.transform(test.data)

svm = LinearSVC(C=1.0).fit(X_tr, train.target)             # liblinear: linear in n, loves sparse input
print("test accuracy:", round(svm.score(X_te, test.target), 3))   # 0.876, trained in 0.09 s
python
import time
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.naive_bayes import MultinomialNB
for name, m in [("LinearSVC", LinearSVC(C=1)), ("LogReg", LogisticRegression(C=10, max_iter=2000)),
                ("MultinomialNB", MultinomialNB()), ("SVC rbf", SVC(kernel="rbf"))]:
    t = time.time(); m.fit(X_tr, train.target)
    print(f"{name:14s} acc={m.score(X_te, test.target):.3f}  fit={time.time()-t:.2f}s")

06 · Career corner

The interview classic

3min
In job descriptions

Roles: Machine Learning Engineer, NLP Engineer, Data Scientist, Research Engineer (bioinformatics / signal processing). JDs rarely say "SVM" in 2026; they say "strong fundamentals in classical ML", "text classification", "anomaly detection". Interviewers, however, love SVMs because the answer reveals whether you understand margins, regularisation and kernels, three ideas that reappear in deep learning (margin losses, weight decay, attention as kernel similarity).

Q: What is the kernel trick?

The SVM dual uses the data only through dot products. Replacing x·x' with a kernel K(x, x') is equivalent to a dot product in a higher-dimensional feature space, without computing the mapping. RBF corresponds to an infinite-dimensional space.

Q: What happens as C → ∞? As gamma → ∞?

C → ∞: hard margin, no violations allowed, boundary hugs the training data, overfits (may fail if not separable). gamma → ∞: each point's influence shrinks to itself, boundary becomes islands around points, overfits.

Q: SVM vs logistic regression?

Both linear classifiers; LR minimises log-loss (probabilistic, every point contributes), SVM minimises hinge loss (only margin violators contribute) and maximises margin. SVM kernels give non-linearity; LR gives calibrated probabilities. Similar accuracy on most tabular data.

07 · Quiz

Three questions, thirty seconds each

3min

You delete a training point that is far from the boundary and retrain the SVM. The boundary…

Its hinge loss was zero and its α was zero: it was not a support vector. Only support vectors define the boundary.

Your RBF-SVM has 100% training accuracy and 61% test accuracy. First thing to try?

Islands around single points = gamma too large (or C too large). Lower gamma widens each point's influence and smooths the boundary.

You have 500k short reviews as TF-IDF vectors. Which classifier?

Kernel SVC builds an n×n kernel matrix: impossible at 500k rows. Linear SVMs train in linear time, love sparse input, and are usually as accurate on text.
