How a machine learns

01 · Hook

"What salary should I ask for?"

Every one of you will face this question in an HR round within months. Thirty people told us their years of experience and their salary. Draw the line, read off your number. But which line? Put TensorFlow Playground on the projector, press play, and watch the loss fall: the same mechanism you will build by hand today, on a harder problem.

5min
The story

Riya's counter-offer

Riya, ECE 2025, gets an offer of 4.2 LPA. Her senior says "ask for more". More how much? She collects thirty data points from friends and seniors (experience, LPA) and plots them. The cloud clearly slopes upward. A line through it would let her say: "the market rate for my experience is about X". That line has two numbers, a slope and an intercept, and there are infinitely many candidates. Picking the best one from the data, automatically, is the first machine-learning algorithm ever written, and it is what we build today.

Everything later in this course (logistic regression, trees, neural networks, LLMs) changes what the "line" looks like. The way it is found stays the same.

Framing for today

A machine "learns" when it adjusts numbers inside itself to reduce a measured error. That is the whole definition. Today the numbers are two (w, b), the error is mean squared error, and the adjustment rule is gradient descent. On Day 19 the numbers become millions and the rule gets a new name, backpropagation, but it is the same rule.

02 · Concept

Guess, measure, nudge, repeat

Four ideas: a hypothesis (the shape of the answer), a cost (how wrong we are), a gradient (which way is downhill), and a learning rate (how big a step to take).

18min

1. Hypothesis space: what shapes are we allowed to guess?

Before learning anything, we decide the form of the answer. Today: a straight line, ŷ = w·x + b. Every choice of w (slope) and b (intercept) is one line; the set of all such lines is the hypothesis space. Learning means searching that space for the line with the lowest cost. A bigger hypothesis space (curves, neural nets) can fit more shapes but is harder to search and easier to overfit; Day 05 is about that trade-off.

2. Cost: one number that says how wrong the line is

🎯

The Zomato delivery-time analogy

Zomato promises "32 minutes" and the order arrives in 40. Error: 8. Another arrives 5 minutes early: error −5. If you just add the errors, +8 and −5 partly cancel and the app looks better than it is. So you square each error (64 and 25, both positive, and the big miss hurts more than the small one), and take the mean over all deliveries. That is mean squared error. A better time-prediction model is one with lower MSE. Our line is a prediction model for salary; its MSE is the average squared gap between the line and the thirty dots.

3. Gradient descent: the blindfolded hill walk

⛰️

Fog on the hill

You are on a hillside in thick fog; you cannot see the valley, but you can feel the slope under your feet. Take a step in the steepest downhill direction. Feel the slope again. Step again. The gradient is the slope under your feet (which direction, how steep); descent is stepping against it; the learning rate is your stride length. Tiny strides: you get there, eventually, in ten thousand steps. Huge strides: you leap across the valley to the opposite hillside, higher than you started, and the next leap is worse. That is what the slider below lets you feel.

Common confusion

"Gradient descent is the model." No. The model is the line (w, b). The cost is how we score a line. Gradient descent is one of several ways to search for the lowest-cost line; for a straight line there is even a closed-form formula (the normal equation). We teach descent because it is the only method that still works when the model has a billion parameters.

4. Why the learning rate matters: the bowl

Fix b and look at cost as a function of w alone. It is a bowl (a parabola), because cost is a sum of squares. The gradient at any point is the slope of the bowl there: large far from the bottom, zero at the bottom. So gradient descent automatically takes big steps when far away and small steps when close, if the learning rate is sane.

The loss curve is the ECG of training. Computed live in this page on the same thirty points: α = 0.002 crawls (still far from the floor at 100 epochs), α = 0.02 converges within a few dozen epochs, α = 0.05 diverges within ten (clipped to the top of the chart). When you train anything from Day 18 onwards, the first thing you look at is this curve, and the first knob you turn is α.

Key idea

Loss going down smoothly: good. Loss flat: α too small or you are done. Loss jumping up or NaN: α too large. Loss down on training but up on validation: overfitting (Day 05). That one curve diagnoses most training problems.

Interview answer

"How does gradient descent work?" — Start with random parameters, compute the cost on the data, compute the gradient of the cost with respect to each parameter (the direction that increases cost fastest), then move each parameter a small step in the opposite direction, scaled by the learning rate. Repeat until the cost stops improving. The learning rate trades speed for stability: too small is slow, too large overshoots and diverges.

03 · Deeper

The math, decoded symbol by symbol

Four formulas. Each one is followed by a sentence that says what every symbol means. If you can read these, Day 19's backpropagation is the same four formulas with a chain rule inside.

10min

Hypothesis

ŷi = w·xi + b

The prediction ŷ (read "y-hat") for example i is the input xi times the weight w plus the bias b. The two amber symbols are the only things the machine is allowed to change.

Cost (mean squared error)

J(w, b) = 1/(2m) · Σi=1..m (ŷi − yi)²

Cost J is a function of the parameters. For each of the m examples, take the gap between prediction and truth, square it, add them all up, divide by m (mean) and by 2. The 2 is cosmetic: it cancels the 2 that appears when we differentiate a square, so the gradient looks cleaner. It does not change where the minimum is.

Gradients (which way is uphill)

∂J/∂w = 1/m · Σ (ŷi − yi) · xi      ∂J/∂b = 1/m · Σ (ŷi − yi)

The partial derivative of J with respect to w says how much J changes if w changes a little. Read it as: "average of (error × input)". If the errors are mostly positive where x is large, increasing w would make things worse, so the gradient is positive, so we should decrease w. The bias gradient is just the average error: if we are predicting too high on average, lower b. In NumPy the two lines are (err * x).mean() and err.mean().

Update rule (step downhill)

w := w − α · ∂J/∂w      b := b − α · ∂J/∂b      (both at once, using the old values)

Move each parameter against its gradient by a fraction α (alpha, the learning rate). The minus sign is what makes it descent. Compute both gradients from the current (w, b) before updating either; updating w and then using the new w to compute b's gradient is a subtle, common bug.

Feature scaling: why the bowl's shape matters

With two parameters the cost is a bowl in 3-D. If x ranges 0–10 while the intercept direction "ranges" 0–1, the bowl is a long narrow valley: steep across, flat along. One learning rate cannot suit both: small enough for the steep wall means crawling along the valley floor. Standardising x (x' = (x − mean) / std) makes the bowl round, so any sensible α works and convergence is 10–100× faster. That is why every sklearn pipeline starts with StandardScaler and why the demo above tolerates α ≈ 2 once you tick the box. In the demo's data, the safe limit is about α < 2/λmax where λmax ≈ 45 for raw x (so α < 0.045) and ≈ 1 after scaling (so α < 2).

The closed form (for a line only)

w = Σ (xi − x̄)(yi − ȳ) / Σ (xi − x̄)²      b = ȳ − w·x̄

For simple linear regression you can solve for the minimum directly: the slope is the covariance of x and y divided by the variance of x, and the intercept makes the line pass through the mean point (x̄, ȳ). sklearn.linear_model.LinearRegression uses the matrix version of this (least squares via SVD), not gradient descent. Gradient descent earns its keep when there is no closed form or the data is too big for one.

How to say this in an interview

"Linear regression assumes ŷ = wx + b and picks w, b to minimise mean squared error. The gradient of MSE is the average of error times input for w, and the average error for b; gradient descent subtracts α times these each iteration. I standardise features first so the loss surface is well-conditioned and a single learning rate works, and I watch the loss curve: smooth decrease means it is working, oscillation or NaN means α is too high."

04 · Code

Build: gradient descent from scratch, then sklearn

Twelve lines of NumPy that learn. Full runnable version with the animated loss curve: open Day_04_How_Machines_Learn.ipynb in Google Colab.

12min

Snippet 1 — data and the cost function

python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
x = rng.uniform(0, 10, 30)                       # years of experience
y = 3 + 1.8 * x + rng.normal(0, 1.2, 30)         # salary in LPA (true line + noise)

def predict(x, w, b):
    return w * x + b                             # hypothesis, vectorised over all 30 points

def cost(x, y, w, b):
    err = predict(x, w, b) - y
    return (err ** 2).mean() / 2                   # J = 1/(2m) Σ (ŷ − y)²

print(cost(x, y, 0, 0), cost(x, y, 1.8, 3))         # bad line vs true line: ~100 vs ~0.7

Snippet 2 — gradient descent, the whole algorithm

python
def gradient_descent(x, y, lr=0.01, epochs=200):
    w, b = 0.0, 0.0                                # start anywhere
    history = []
    for epoch in range(epochs):
        err = predict(x, w, b) - y                   # (ŷ − y) for every point, shape (30,)
        dw = (err * x).mean()                        # ∂J/∂w = mean(error · x)
        db = err.mean()                              # ∂J/∂b = mean(error)
        w -= lr * dw                                 # step downhill
        b -= lr * db                                 # (dw, db both computed from the OLD w, b)
        history.append(cost(x, y, w, b))
    return w, b, history

w, b, hist = gradient_descent(x, y, lr=0.01)
print(f"w={w:.3f}  b={b:.3f}  final cost={hist[-1]:.3f}")   # → w≈1.9, b≈2.5 after 200 epochs; keeps improving

Snippet 3 — three ways to get the same line

python
w_cf = ((x - x.mean()) * (y - y.mean())).sum() / ((x - x.mean()) ** 2).sum()
b_cf = y.mean() - w_cf * x.mean()
print(w_cf, b_cf)                    # the exact minimum, no iterations: ≈ 1.736, 3.345
python · what you'll use at work
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
X = x.reshape(-1, 1)                  # sklearn wants a 2-D matrix: (n_samples, n_features)
model = LinearRegression().fit(X, y)  # least squares under the hood (SVD), not GD
print(model.coef_, model.intercept_)  # [1.736] 3.345 — identical to the closed form
model.predict([[2.0]])               # Riya, 2 years: ≈ 6.8 LPA
model.score(X, y)                     # R² (Day 12): fraction of variance explained
python · gradient descent, but sklearn's
from sklearn.linear_model import SGDRegressor
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
sgd = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(),                      # scale first: SGD needs it
                    SGDRegressor(learning_rate="constant", eta0=0.01, max_iter=1000, random_state=0))
sgd.fit(X, y)
print(sgd.predict([[2.0]]))                              # ≈ 6.8 again; eta0 is α

Snippet 4 — feature scaling, and why the notebook's α = 0.5 works

python
x_s = (x - x.mean()) / x.std()                        # standardise: mean 0, std 1
w_s, b_s, hist_s = gradient_descent(x_s, y, lr=0.5, epochs=30)   # 50× larger α, 7× fewer epochs, converged
print(hist_s[-1])                                     # ≈ 0.47, the floor

# to predict for a raw x, scale it the same way (with the TRAINING mean/std!)
riya = (2.0 - x.mean()) / x.std()
print(predict(riya, w_s, b_s))                         # ≈ 6.8 LPA
Key idea

Look at how little code it is: a predict, a cost, a gradient, an update. On Day 19 predict becomes a stack of matrix multiplies and dw comes from the chain rule, but the loop is unchanged. If you understand Snippet 2 you understand what optimizer.step() does in PyTorch.

06 · Career corner

"Explain gradient descent" is interview question number one

3min
In job descriptions

Machine Learning Engineer, Data Scientist, Applied Scientist (Junior). The JD says "solid understanding of ML fundamentals: linear models, loss functions, optimisation". The interviewer's first technical question, in a large fraction of fresher ML interviews, is some form of "explain gradient descent" or "what happens if the learning rate is too high". They are not testing memory; they are checking whether you can explain a mechanism simply, because that is what you will do in every design review.

Interview questions you can now answer

  • "What happens if the learning rate is too high / too low?" — Too high: steps overshoot the minimum, loss oscillates or diverges to NaN. Too low: convergence is painfully slow and may stall. Fix: scale features, sweep α on a log scale, watch the loss curve.
  • "Why MSE and not mean absolute error?" — MSE is smooth (differentiable everywhere) so the gradient is clean, and it penalises large errors more. MAE is more robust to outliers. Choice depends on whether big misses should hurt disproportionately.
  • "Batch vs stochastic vs mini-batch?" — Batch uses all data per update (exact, slow per step); stochastic uses one example (noisy, cheap); mini-batch uses 32–512 (noise averaged, GPU-efficient) and is the default for deep learning.

07 · Quiz

Three quick checks

3min

Your loss curve goes 100 → 40 → 90 → 400 → NaN. Most likely cause?

Each step overshoots to a higher point on the opposite wall of the bowl; the oscillation grows until numbers overflow. Lower α or scale the features.

∂J/∂b for MSE equals…

Differentiating (wx + b − y)² with respect to b gives 2(ŷ − y); the 1/(2m) turns the sum into the plain mean of the errors. The error × x version is ∂J/∂w.

Standardising x before gradient descent mainly…

Scaling reshapes the loss surface, not the optimum's predictions (you can map w, b back). It does not remove outliers. It conditions the problem so larger learning rates are stable.
