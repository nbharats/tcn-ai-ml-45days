01 · Hook

"Why was my loan rejected?"

A customer walks into a bank branch in Indore with that question. The branch manager cannot answer "because θ₇ was −0.34". She needs a sentence.

5min

Lending is the most regulated use of machine learning in India. Credit decisions must be explainable to the customer, auditable by the bank's risk committee, and defensible to the regulator. Yesterday's Naive Bayes multiplied 5,000 word probabilities; last week's Ridge regression mixed 79 features into one weighted sum. Neither gives the branch manager a sentence. A decision tree does: "Your credit score was below 650 and you have been in your current job for less than 12 months." Two questions, one reason, done. That is why trees (and the forests and boosted trees built on them) are the workhorse of tabular data in banking, insurance, telecom and e-commerce, and why "explainable ML" is now a phrase in job descriptions.

A tree is also how you already think. Deciding whether to order in tonight: "Is it raining? → Is there dal at home? → Is it past 9 pm?" Each question splits the situations into purer groups until every group has one obvious answer. Today's algorithm is just a machine that finds the best question to ask first, then the best question after that, by measuring how much each question cleans up the mess.

02 · Concept

Ask the question that cleans up the most mess

Three ideas: impurity is mess, a split is a question, and greedily asking the best question at each step grows a tree.

18min

Idea 1 · Impurity: how mixed is this group?

Take 100 loan applicants: 50 repaid, 50 defaulted. That group is maximally impure; you learn nothing by knowing someone is in it. Take another group: 98 repaid, 2 defaulted. Almost pure; "approve" is a safe answer. We need a number for this. Two are standard: Gini impurity G = 1 − Σ pk² (the probability that two random members of the group have different labels) and entropy H = −Σ pk log₂ pk (the average number of yes/no questions needed to identify a member's label). Both are 0 when the group is pure and maximal at a 50/50 split. In practice they pick nearly the same splits; sklearn defaults to Gini because it skips the logarithm.

🧺

Sorting the laundry

A bucket with whites and colours mixed 50/50 is pure chaos: pick any two items and they probably do not match. A bucket that is 95% whites is almost sorted. Gini is literally "the chance that two socks pulled at random do not match". A good sorting question ("is it white?") produces two buckets that are each much more uniform than the original. A useless question ("was it bought on a Tuesday?") produces two buckets exactly as mixed as before. The tree's job is to find the sock-sorting questions and ignore the Tuesday questions.

Key idea · Information gain

A split's quality is gain = impurity(parent) − weighted average impurity(children), where each child is weighted by its share of the samples. A question that sends 990 people left and 10 right can only gain a little even if the 10 are perfectly pure; a question that splits 500/500 into two pure groups gains everything. The tree tries every feature and every threshold between sorted values and keeps the one with the largest gain. That is the whole algorithm (CART).

Common confusion

Trees do not need feature scaling, one-hot encoding of ordinal features, or any distance metric. A threshold "income ≥ 40,000" is the same question whether income is in rupees or lakhs. That is a real advantage over KNN and logistic regression, and it is why trees handle messy tabular data so well.

Idea 2 · Find the best split, then do it again: the live lab

Below is a loan dataset with two features. Drag the split threshold by hand and watch Gini and gain update; press Find best split to watch the algorithm sweep every candidate threshold on both features and stop at the winner. Then turn up depth to let it recurse into each child, and watch the regions fragment.

Idea 3 · Trees overfit by default, so we prune

Left alone, CART keeps splitting until every leaf is pure, which on real data means one training row per leaf. It has memorised, not learned; sklearn's default max_depth=None does exactly this. Two families of cure:

  • Pre-pruning (stop early): max_depth, min_samples_leaf, min_samples_split, min_impurity_decrease. Simple, fast, and what the sliders above do.
  • Post-pruning (grow fully, then cut back): cost-complexity pruning with ccp_alpha. It charges the tree α per leaf and removes any branch whose accuracy gain is not worth its leaves. One knob, chosen by cross-validation, and usually better than guessing a depth.
🌳

The mango tree and the mali

An unpruned mango tree grows a branch toward every patch of sunlight, including the ones over the neighbour's wall that will never bear fruit. The mali cuts branches whose fruit is not worth the water they drink. ccp_alpha is the price of water: raise it and only the productive branches survive.

Interview answer · "How does a decision tree decide where to split?"

"At each node it considers every feature and every threshold between adjacent sorted values, computes the impurity of the two resulting children, Gini or entropy, and picks the split with the largest information gain, i.e. the largest drop in weighted impurity. It repeats greedily on each child until a stopping rule (depth, min samples, purity) is hit. Because greedy growth overfits, we prune with cost-complexity pruning chosen by cross-validation."

03 · Deeper

Entropy, Gini, gain, pruning and importance in formulas

Everything the whiteboard round will ask, with numbers you can compute by hand.

10min

1 · Impurity measures

Gini(S) = 1 − Σk pk²   Entropy(S) = −Σk pk log₂ pk

S is the set of samples at a node, pk the fraction belonging to class k. Worked example: 30 repaid, 10 defaulted → p = (0.75, 0.25). Gini = 1 − (0.5625 + 0.0625) = 0.375. Entropy = −(0.75·log₂0.75 + 0.25·log₂0.25) = 0.311 + 0.5 = 0.811 bits. For regression trees the impurity is simply the variance (MSE) of the targets in the node, and leaves predict the mean.

2 · Information gain

Gain(S, split) = I(S) − Σchild c (|Sc| / |S|) · I(Sc)

I is Gini or entropy, |Sc|/|S| the fraction of samples going to child c. Continue the example: split the 40 into left (25 repaid, 1 defaulted; Gini 0.074) and right (5 repaid, 9 defaulted; Gini 0.459). Weighted child Gini = (26/40)·0.074 + (14/40)·0.459 = 0.048 + 0.161 = 0.209. Gain = 0.375 − 0.209 = 0.166. The tree computes this for every candidate and keeps the max. With n samples and d features that is O(n·d) candidates per node (after one sort per feature), which is why trees train fast.

3 · Cost-complexity pruning

Rα(T) = R(T) + α · |leaves(T)|

R(T) is the tree's total training impurity (or misclassification), |leaves(T)| its number of leaves, α ≥ 0 the price per leaf. For α = 0 the full tree wins; as α grows, subtrees whose impurity reduction per leaf is below α are collapsed, one at a time, giving a nested sequence of smaller trees. sklearn's cost_complexity_pruning_path() returns the exact α values where each collapse happens; you cross-validate over them and pass the winner as ccp_alpha.

4 · Feature importance (and its trap)

importance(f) = Σnodes splitting on f (|Snode| / n) · Gainnode, normalised to sum to 1.

A feature's importance is the total weighted impurity it removed across the tree. It is free, useful, and biased: features with many unique values (a continuous "age" vs a binary "gender") get more chances to split and inflate their score, and correlated features share credit arbitrarily. For serious work, cross-check with permutation_importance (shuffle a column, measure the accuracy drop) or SHAP (Day 10).

How to say this in an interview

"A decision tree recursively partitions the feature space with axis-aligned thresholds chosen greedily to maximise information gain, measured by the drop in Gini impurity or entropy. Unconstrained trees overfit, so I limit depth or leaf size, or grow fully and post-prune with cost-complexity pruning, selecting ccp_alpha by cross-validation. Trees need no scaling, handle mixed features, and are interpretable, but a single tree is high-variance, which is exactly what ensembles fix."

04 · Code

Grow it, read it, overfit it, prune it

Full runnable version on the Titanic data, with plots: open Day_08_Decision_Trees.ipynb in Google Colab.

12min

1 · Train and read a tree (Titanic survival)

python
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier, export_text, plot_tree
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

df = pd.read_csv("https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mwaskom/seaborn-data/master/titanic.csv")
df = df[["survived", "pclass", "sex", "age", "sibsp", "parch", "fare"]].dropna()
df["sex"] = (df.sex == "male").astype(int)          # trees are fine with 0/1; no scaling needed
X, y = df.drop(columns="survived"), df.survived
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, random_state=42, stratify=y)

tree = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=3, random_state=42).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
print(export_text(tree, feature_names=list(X.columns)))   # the tree as readable if/else rules
print("train:", tree.score(X_tr, y_tr), " test:", tree.score(X_te, y_te))

2 · Draw it

python
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
plt.figure(figsize=(18, 7))
plot_tree(tree, feature_names=list(X.columns), class_names=["died", "survived"],
          filled=True, rounded=True, impurity=True, proportion=True, fontsize=9)
plt.show()
# each box: the question, gini, share of samples, class proportions, majority class
# the first question is sex <= 0.5, i.e. "female?" — "women and children first" learned from data

3 · Overfit on purpose, then look at the damage

python
full = DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=42).fit(X_tr, y_tr)        # max_depth=None: grows until leaves are pure
print("depth:", full.get_depth(), " leaves:", full.get_n_leaves())
print("train:", full.score(X_tr, y_tr), " test:", full.score(X_te, y_te))   # ~0.99 vs ~0.70: memorised

for d in [1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, None]:                      # pre-pruning sweep
    t = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=d, random_state=42).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
    print(f"depth={str(d):4s} leaves={t.get_n_leaves():3d} train={t.score(X_tr, y_tr):.3f} test={t.score(X_te, y_te):.3f}")

4 · Post-pruning with cost-complexity (ccp_alpha)

import numpy as np
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score

path = full.cost_complexity_pruning_path(X_tr, y_tr)      # every alpha at which a branch would be cut
alphas = path.ccp_alphas[:-1]                              # drop the last one (it prunes to a single leaf)

cv = [cross_val_score(DecisionTreeClassifier(ccp_alpha=a, random_state=42), X_tr, y_tr, cv=5).mean() for a in alphas]
best_alpha = alphas[int(np.argmax(cv))]
pruned = DecisionTreeClassifier(ccp_alpha=best_alpha, random_state=42).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
print(f"best alpha={best_alpha:.4f} leaves={pruned.get_n_leaves()} test={pruned.score(X_te, y_te):.3f}")
# impurities rise as alpha rises; leaves fall. Plot both to see the trade-off.
leaves = [DecisionTreeClassifier(ccp_alpha=a, random_state=42).fit(X_tr, y_tr).get_n_leaves() for a in alphas]
fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 4))
ax[0].plot(alphas, leaves, marker="o"); ax[0].set_xlabel("ccp_alpha"); ax[0].set_ylabel("leaves")
ax[1].plot(alphas, cv, marker="o");     ax[1].set_xlabel("ccp_alpha"); ax[1].set_ylabel("5-fold CV accuracy")
plt.show()

5 · Feature importance, two ways

python
from sklearn.inspection import permutation_importance

imp = pd.Series(pruned.feature_importances_, index=X.columns).sort_values(ascending=False)
print("impurity-based:\n", imp.round(3))                 # fast, biased toward high-cardinality features

perm = permutation_importance(pruned, X_te, y_te, n_repeats=20, random_state=42)
print("permutation (test set):\n", pd.Series(perm.importances_mean, index=X.columns).sort_values(ascending=False).round(3))
# if a feature is important here, shuffling it hurt accuracy on data the tree never saw
Gotchas you will hit

Different tree every run? Ties between equally good splits are broken randomly; set random_state. Tree ignores your one-hot columns? Trees handle them, but with many categories a single binary split per level is weak; consider ordinal codes or target encoding (Day 17). plot_tree unreadable? Plot a max_depth=3 tree for the slide and use export_text for the real one. Want prettier plots: dtreeviz ↗ (pip install dtreeviz).

06 · Career corner

How today shows up in a job

3min
In job descriptions

Credit Risk Modeller / Analyst (banks, NBFCs, fintech lenders), Data Scientist (tabular problems: churn, pricing, fraud) and ML Engineer roles list "tree-based models", "XGBoost/LightGBM", "model interpretability / explainable AI", "SHAP". Single trees are the interview topic; ensembles of trees are the production model. You cannot explain a Random Forest tomorrow if you cannot explain one tree today.

Real interview questions

  • "Gini vs entropy: when does it matter?" → Almost never for accuracy; both peak at 50/50 and are zero at purity. Gini is faster (no log), so it is the default; entropy slightly favours balanced splits.
  • "How do you stop a decision tree from overfitting?" → Pre-pruning (max_depth, min_samples_leaf), post-pruning (ccp_alpha via cost-complexity path + CV), or use an ensemble that averages many trees.
  • "Why doesn't a tree need feature scaling?" → Splits are thresholds on one feature at a time; any monotonic transform of a feature gives the same set of possible splits.

07 · Quiz

Three quick checks

3min

A node has 40 repaid and 40 defaulted. Its Gini impurity is…

Gini = 1 − (0.5² + 0.5²) = 1 − 0.5 = 0.5, the maximum for two classes. (Entropy would be 1 bit.)

Your tree scores 99% on training data and 71% on test. The single most direct fix is…

That gap is overfitting (high variance). Scaling does nothing for trees; the criterion barely matters. Pruning, or an ensemble, is the fix.

Raising ccp_alpha from 0 to 0.02 will…

ccp_alpha is the price per leaf in cost-complexity pruning; a higher price collapses branches whose gain is not worth their leaves.
