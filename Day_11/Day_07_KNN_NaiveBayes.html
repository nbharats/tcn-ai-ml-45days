Lazy learners and probabilistic learners: KNN & Naive Bayes.

Two models that learn nothing like the line-fitters of the last three days. K-Nearest Neighbours refuses to learn at all: it memorises the data and, when asked, looks at whoever is standing closest. Naive Bayes learns by counting, then reasons like a detective with Bayes' theorem. One will teach you why feature scaling and dimensionality matter; the other will give you a spam filter that runs in a millisecond. Both are in every interviewer's pocket.

01 · Hook

The oldest ML you use every day is a word-counter from 1998

Before deep learning, before Gmail, a handful of researchers noticed that the word "FREE" appears in spam 40 times more often than in real mail. That observation, plus a 260-year-old formula, killed most spam on the internet.

5min

Naive Bayes spam filters shipped in 1998 (Sahami et al. at Microsoft Research), went mainstream in 2002 with Paul Graham's essay A Plan for Spam, and are still the first line inside most mail systems today because they are absurdly cheap: one pass over the words, a few multiplications, done. Your SMS app's "Promotions / OTP / Personal" folders, Gmail's spam tab, and the "Is this review fake?" filter on Flipkart all start with this idea.

The second model today is even simpler to explain. Ask a Swiggy delivery partner whether a new restaurant will get repeat orders. They will not fit a regression. They will say: "The three restaurants nearest to it, same price band, same cuisine, all do well. So yes." That is K-Nearest Neighbours. No training. No equation. Just: who are you standing next to? And despite the simplicity, KNN is the ancestor of every recommendation system ("customers like you also bought…") and of the vector databases you meet in Week 6.

02 · Concept

Ask the neighbours; count the evidence

Two mechanisms, two live labs. Click, slide, type.

18min

Part A · K-Nearest Neighbours: a model with no training step

Every model so far had a fit phase (find θ) and a predict phase (apply θ). KNN skips the first entirely. Its "training" is self.X = X; self.y = y. All the work happens at prediction time: measure the distance from the new point to every stored point, take the k closest, let them vote (classification) or average (regression). That is why it is called a lazy learner: it postpones all effort until it is asked, and it never forgets a single example.

🏘️

New in the neighbourhood

You shift to a new flat in Pune and want to know if the area is veg-friendly. You do not run a survey of the city; you ask the five nearest neighbours. If four say "mostly veg", that is your answer. Three things decide whether this works: how many neighbours you ask (k), how you define "near" (distance metric), and whether "near" is measured fairly. If one neighbour is 50 metres away but 30 floors up, is that near? That last question is feature scaling, and KNN dies without it.

Key idea · k is a bias-variance knob

Small k = low bias, high variance (one noisy neighbour flips the answer). Large k = high bias, low variance (everything becomes the majority class). Pick k by cross-validation, and use an odd k for two classes so votes cannot tie. The leave-one-out accuracy shown above is exactly that search, done live.

Common confusion · "KNN is fast because there is no training"

Backwards. Training is free, but every prediction costs O(n·d): compare against all n stored points in d dimensions. With 10 million users and 300 features that is impossible in real time, which is why production systems use approximate nearest-neighbour indexes (FAISS, HNSW, Annoy; Day 38). Also: the whole training set must stay in memory forever.

Part B · Naive Bayes: classification by counting

Bayes' theorem lets you flip a conditional probability. You can easily count how often the word "lottery" appears in spam, P(lottery | spam). What you want is the other direction, P(spam | lottery). The theorem says: P(spam | words) ∝ P(words | spam) · P(spam). The prior P(spam) is just the fraction of spam in your inbox. The likelihood P(words | spam) would need a table of every possible message; impossible. So we make the naive assumption: words are independent given the class, so P(words | spam) = P(w₁|spam) · P(w₂|spam) · …, and each factor is just a count. Wrong in reality ("click" and "here" are not independent), but it works shockingly well because we only need the ranking of classes to be right, not the exact probability.

🕵️

The CID inspector with a notebook

An inspector has a notebook: "Of the last 100 fraud calls, 90 mentioned 'KYC', 70 said 'urgent', 5 said 'beta' (son). Of 100 genuine calls: 3 said 'KYC', 10 'urgent', 60 'beta'." A new call says "urgent KYC, beta". The inspector does not consider how the words interact; he multiplies: fraud score = 0.9 × 0.7 × 0.05, genuine score = 0.03 × 0.1 × 0.6, times the base rate of fraud calls. Whichever product is bigger wins. That is the entire algorithm. The notebook is the training; the multiplication is the prediction.

Interview answer · "Why is it called naive?"

"Because it assumes the features are conditionally independent given the class, so the joint likelihood factorises into a product of per-feature likelihoods we can estimate by counting. The assumption is false for real text, but the classifier still ranks classes correctly most of the time, trains in one pass, and works with tiny data, which is why it remains the baseline for spam, sentiment and document classification."

03 · Deeper

Distances, the curse, and the naive trick in formulas

Everything you need to defend both models on a whiteboard.

10min

1 · Distance metrics

Euclidean: d(a,b) = √( Σi (ai − bi)² )   Manhattan: d = Σi |ai − bi|   Minkowski: d = ( Σi |ai − bi|p )1/p

a, b are two points, i runs over features. Minkowski with p = 2 is Euclidean (straight-line, "as the crow flies"), p = 1 is Manhattan (grid-walking, like Delhi's blocks, robust to a single wild feature). sklearn's default is p=2. For text and embeddings, cosine distance (1 − cos θ between the vectors) is used instead because length should not matter. All of them assume features share a scale, hence StandardScaler or MinMaxScaler before KNN, always.

2 · Choosing k, and the cost of a prediction

Prediction for one query: compute n distances of d terms each, then find the k smallest: O(n·d + n log k). Trees (algorithm="kd_tree", "ball_tree") speed this up for low d; sklearn's "auto" picks for you. k is chosen by cross-validation; the classic starting guess is k ≈ √n, odd. weights="distance" lets closer neighbours vote louder, which softens large k.

3 · The curse of dimensionality

In d dimensions, the volume of a unit cube is 1 but the volume of the inscribed sphere shrinks toward 0 as d grows; almost all of the space is in the corners, far from everything. Concretely, for random points the ratio (dmax − dmin) / dmin → 0 as d → ∞: your nearest neighbour is barely nearer than your farthest. The chart above measured it. Practical rule: KNN is happy with d ≲ 20 meaningful features; beyond that, reduce dimensions or switch models.

4 · Bayes' theorem and the naive factorisation

P(y | x) = P(x | y) · P(y) / P(x)   and with naivety   P(y | x₁…xd) ∝ P(y) · Πi P(xi | y)

y the class (spam/ham), x the features (words). P(y) is the prior (class frequency). P(x | y) the likelihood. P(x) the evidence, identical for every class so we drop it and compare the numerators: ŷ = argmaxy P(y) Πi P(xi|y). In code we sum logs instead of multiplying (1,000 probabilities multiplied underflow to 0.0).

5 · Two likelihoods you will actually use

Multinomial NB (word counts): P(w | spam) = (count(w, spam) + α) / (total words in spam + α·V). V is vocabulary size, α the Laplace/Lidstone smoothing (sklearn alpha=1.0). Without α, one unseen word gives probability 0 and kills the whole product; that is the single most common NB bug. Gaussian NB (continuous features): assume each feature within a class is a bell curve: P(xi | y) = N(xi; μiy, σ²iy), with mean and variance estimated per feature per class. Bernoulli NB uses word present/absent instead of counts.

How to say this in an interview

"KNN is a non-parametric, instance-based method: no training, predictions by majority vote of the k nearest scaled points; k controls bias-variance and it degrades in high dimensions because distances concentrate. Naive Bayes is a generative model that applies Bayes' theorem under a conditional-independence assumption, estimating per-class likelihoods by counting with Laplace smoothing; it is a strong, fast baseline for text."

12min

1 · KNN with scaling, and a k sweep

from sklearn.datasets import load_iris
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split, cross_val_score
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

X, y = load_iris(return_X_y=True)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.3, random_state=42, stratify=y)

for k in [1, 3, 5, 11, 21, 51]:
    knn = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=k))   # scaler INSIDE the pipeline
    cv = cross_val_score(knn, X_tr, y_tr, cv=5).mean()                         # pick k on training folds only
    print(f"k={k:2d}  cv accuracy={cv:.3f}")

best = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=5, weights="distance")).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
print("test accuracy:", best.score(X_te, y_te))
import numpy as np
from collections import Counter

def knn_predict(X_train, y_train, x_query, k=5):
    d = np.sqrt(((X_train - x_query) ** 2).sum(axis=1))     # Euclidean distance to every stored point
    nearest = np.argsort(d)[:k]                             # indices of the k smallest
    votes = Counter(y_train[nearest])                       # count labels among them
    return votes.most_common(1)[0][0]                        # majority label

# scale first, or the widest feature decides everything
sc = StandardScaler().fit(X_tr)
preds = [knn_predict(sc.transform(X_tr), y_tr, xq, k=5) for xq in sc.transform(X_te)]
print("scratch accuracy:", np.mean(np.array(preds) == y_te))

2 · Gaussian Naive Bayes on continuous features

python
from sklearn.naive_bayes import GaussianNB

gnb = GaussianNB().fit(X_tr, y_tr)                 # no scaling needed: it models each feature's own bell curve
print("GaussianNB test accuracy:", gnb.score(X_te, y_te))
print("class priors P(y):", gnb.class_prior_.round(3))     # learned from class frequencies
print("per-class feature means:", gnb.theta_.round(2))  # shape (3 classes, 4 features): μ_iy
print("per-class feature variances:", gnb.var_.round(2)) # σ²_iy: that is the whole "model"

3 · A spam filter: CountVectorizer + MultinomialNB

python
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer
from sklearn.naive_bayes import MultinomialNB

# SMS Spam Collection: 5,572 real SMS, labels ham/spam (notebook downloads it, with an offline fallback)
sms = pd.read_csv("sms.tsv", sep="\t", header=None, names=["label", "text"])
ys = (sms.label == "spam").astype(int)
t_tr, t_te, ys_tr, ys_te = train_test_split(sms.text, ys, test_size=0.25, random_state=42, stratify=ys)

spam_clf = make_pipeline(CountVectorizer(lowercase=True), MultinomialNB(alpha=1.0))   # alpha = Laplace smoothing
spam_clf.fit(t_tr, ys_tr)
print("test accuracy:", spam_clf.score(t_te, ys_te))          # ~0.98 on the real dataset
print(spam_clf.predict_proba(["URGENT! Your KYC is pending, click link to avoid account block",
                              "bro are we meeting at the chai stall at 5?"]).round(3))

Gotchas you will hit

KNN accuracy dropped after adding a feature? Probably unscaled, or a useless feature adding noise to distances (KNN cannot ignore features; trees can). NB gives 0.000 or 1.000 probabilities? Normal: the independence assumption makes it overconfident. Rank, don't trust the number. CountVectorizer vs TfidfVectorizer: NB is designed for counts; TF-IDF often still works and is Day 28's topic.

Real interview questions

  • "Why must you scale features for KNN but not for Naive Bayes?" → KNN's distance mixes features, so a wide-range feature dominates; NB models each feature's own distribution per class, so scale is irrelevant.
  • "What happens in Naive Bayes when a test word never appeared in training?" → Its likelihood is 0 and the product collapses; Laplace smoothing (alpha) adds a pseudo-count so every word keeps a small non-zero probability.
  • "KNN on 1,000 features gives bad results. Why, and what would you do?" → Curse of dimensionality: distances concentrate. Reduce with PCA/feature selection, use cosine distance on embeddings, or switch to trees/linear models.

07 · Quiz

Three quick checks

3min

In KNN, moving from k = 1 to k = 51 on a 200-point dataset mainly…

Large k averages over many neighbours: smoother regions, less sensitivity to single noisy points (lower variance), but it blurs real structure (higher bias). Training cost is zero either way.

Your NB spam filter labels every message containing the word "Bengaluru" as ham with probability 1.000, even obvious scams. Most likely cause?

With no smoothing, P("Bengaluru" | spam) = 0, so the spam product is exactly 0 regardless of other words. Set alpha > 0 (sklearn default 1.0).

Which model needs the entire training set in memory at prediction time?

KNN is instance-based: it has no parameters, only the stored examples, and must scan them for every query. NB keeps a small count table; logistic regression keeps one weight per feature.
