01 · Hook

787 wrong guesses that were exactly right

In 1906 the statistician Francis Galton visited a county fair in England where 787 people paid to guess the weight of an ox. Butchers, farmers, clerks, children. Individually they were all wrong. The average of their guesses was 1,197 pounds. The ox weighed 1,198.

5min
Ask the class

Why did the average beat the experts?

Each person's error was partly random: one over-estimated because the ox looked tall, another under-estimated because it was standing far away. Random errors in different directions cancel when you average. The bias that everyone shares (say, everyone ignores that the ox has not eaten) does not cancel. Hold this sentence: averaging kills variance, not bias. Today's whole lesson sits on it.

You already trust crowds. IPL fantasy leagues aggregate lakhs of team picks. Google Maps' "23 minutes" is an average over thousands of drivers' phones. Zomato's 4.2 stars is a vote. A Random Forest is the same trick applied to decision trees.

Yesterday's problem, restated

Day 08's unpruned tree on the Telco churn data scored 99.8% on training and 74.2% on test. Its ROC-AUC was 0.66, barely better than sorting customers at random. A tree is a low-bias, high-variance learner: change 5% of the rows and it grows a completely different shape. Today we turn that weakness into the raw material of a stronger model.

02 · Concept

Bootstrap, bag, vote

Three moves. Resample the data with replacement (bootstrap). Train one tree per resample (bagging = bootstrap aggregating). Let them vote. Random Forest adds a fourth move: at every split, each tree may only look at a random handful of features.

18min
🫖

The chai-tasting panel

A tea estate wants to grade 150 chai samples. One expert taster (a single deep tree) is precise but moody: give her the same 150 cups tomorrow in a different order and her grades shift. So the estate hires 100 tasters. Each taster gets a random tray of 150 cups drawn with replacement from the same 150 (some cups appear twice, about a third never appear on that tray). And each taster is only allowed to judge a random subset of qualities (aroma, colour, strength) per sip, so they cannot all copy the loudest signal. Final grade: majority vote. No single taster is great. The panel is excellent and, crucially, stable.

Watch a forest grow, one tree at a time

Below is a two-class dataset with a wavy boundary and some label noise (the real world). Press Grow 1 tree: the training points that land in this tree's bootstrap sample light up (bigger ring = drawn more than once, faded = left out). The tree is fitted on that sample only and its decision region appears as a small tile on the right. The big panel shows the majority vote of all trees grown so far. Keep pressing and watch the ensemble boundary calm down while the individual tiles stay jagged.

Key idea

Every tree sees a different version of the data and a different subset of features, so every tree makes different mistakes. Mistakes that are different cancel in a vote. Mistakes that are shared do not. Bagging manufactures diversity on purpose.

Common confusion

"More trees will overfit." No. Adding trees to a Random Forest cannot increase variance; the vote converges to a limit as n_estimators → ∞. What overfits is each tree's depth, and even that is mostly tamed by the averaging. The usual rule at work: 200–500 trees, then stop because training time grows linearly.

Why the feature subset matters

If one feature is very strong (like tenure in churn data), every bootstrap tree would split on it first and all trees would look alike. Correlated trees do not cancel each other's errors. max_features='sqrt' forces each split to pick from a random handful, so different trees discover different, weaker signals. That is the difference between plain bagging and a Random Forest.

Bootstrap in 20 cups: who gets left out?

Draw 20 cups with replacement from a tray of 20. Some cups get picked twice or thrice; some not at all. The ones never picked are that tree's out-of-bag (OOB) cups, free test data the tree never saw. Press the button a few times: the left-out share hovers around 37%, because the chance one cup is never picked in 20 draws is (1 − 1/20)20 ≈ e−1 ≈ 0.368.

Two architectures: parallel vs sequential

Interview answer

"Random Forest is bagging of decision trees plus random feature selection at each split. Bootstrapping and feature subsampling de-correlate the trees, so averaging their predictions reduces variance without increasing bias much. It gives a free validation estimate through the out-of-bag score and needs very little tuning, which is why it is a strong default for tabular data."

03 · Deeper

Why averaging works, in one formula

The mechanics are short. Three formulas, each decoded in plain words, then the two practical outputs you will actually use at work: OOB score and feature importance.

10min

1. Variance of an average of correlated trees

Suppose each tree's prediction has variance σ² and any two trees have correlation ρ. The variance of the average of B trees is

Var(average) = ρ·σ² + (1 − ρ)·σ² / B

Decode it: σ² is how wobbly one tree is, B is the number of trees, ρ is how similar the trees are to each other. As B grows the second term vanishes, but the first term ρ·σ² stays. If the trees are identical (ρ = 1), a million of them are no better than one. Bootstrapping lowers ρ a bit; random feature subsets lower it a lot. That is the entire design rationale of Random Forest, and it is why the simulator gains nothing when you switch both tricks off.

2. Out-of-bag: a validation set you did not have to hold out

A bootstrap sample of n rows drawn n times with replacement misses each specific row with probability

P(row left out) = (1 − 1/n)n → 1/e ≈ 0.368

So every tree has ~37% of rows it never saw. To score row i out-of-bag, take only the trees that did not train on i, let them vote, and compare with the true label. Do this for all rows and you get oob_score_: an honest generalisation estimate for free, close to what K-fold cross-validation would give. On Telco churn our 300-tree forest reported OOB accuracy 78.9% and the held-out test accuracy was 79.0%. It is not a replacement for a final test set (you still tune hyper-parameters against it, which leaks a little), but it makes quick experiments cheap.

3. Feature importance: two numbers, one of them lies

Mean decrease in impurity (rf.feature_importances_) sums, over all trees, how much Gini impurity each feature's splits removed, weighted by how many rows reached the split, normalised to 1. It is computed on training data and it is biased toward features with many possible split points: a continuous column or a high-cardinality ID can look important because it offers thousands of places to cut, not because it predicts anything.

Permutation importance (sklearn.inspection.permutation_importance) shuffles one column of the test set, re-scores the trained model, and reports how much the score fell. If shuffling gender changes nothing, gender was not being used, whatever the impurity number said.

How to say this in an interview

"A single tree has high variance. Averaging B trees divides the independent part of that variance by B, but the correlated part ρσ² remains, so Random Forest de-correlates trees with bootstrap rows and random feature subsets per split. Out-of-bag samples, roughly 37% of rows per tree, give a free validation score. For feature importance I prefer permutation importance on held-out data because impurity importance is biased toward high-cardinality features."

04 · Code

Tree vs forest on real churn data

Telco customer churn: 7,043 customers, 20 columns, 26.5% churned. The same dataset returns for Mini Project 1 on Day 17, so this code is an investment. Full runnable version: open Day_09_Ensembles_RandomForest.ipynb in Google Colab.

12min

Snippet 1 · Load and prepare

python
# Telco churn CSV hosted by IBM on GitHub (stable raw URL)
import pandas as pd
url = "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/IBM/telco-customer-churn-on-icp4d/master/data/Telco-Customer-Churn.csv"
df = pd.read_csv(url)

# TotalCharges has 11 blank strings → coerce to NaN, fill with median
df["TotalCharges"] = pd.to_numeric(df["TotalCharges"], errors="coerce")
df["TotalCharges"] = df["TotalCharges"].fillna(df["TotalCharges"].median())

y = (df["Churn"] == "Yes").astype(int)
X = pd.get_dummies(df.drop(columns=["customerID", "Churn"]), drop_first=True).astype(float)
print(X.shape, "churn rate:", y.mean().round(3))   # (7043, 30) churn rate: 0.265

Snippet 2 · One tree vs a forest (the headline result)

python
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score, roc_auc_score

X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.2, stratify=y, random_state=42)

tree = DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=42).fit(X_tr, y_tr)        # unpruned, like Day 08
rf   = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, oob_score=True,      # 300 trees, ask for OOB
                              n_jobs=-1, random_state=42).fit(X_tr, y_tr)

for name, m in [("tree", tree), ("forest", rf)]:
    p = m.predict_proba(X_te)[:, 1]
    print(f"{name:7s} train={m.score(X_tr, y_tr):.3f}  test={accuracy_score(y_te, p > 0.5):.3f}  AUC={roc_auc_score(y_te, p):.3f}")
print("forest OOB score:", round(rf.oob_score_, 3))
# tree    train=0.998  test=0.742  AUC=0.662
# forest  train=0.998  test=0.790  AUC=0.827
# forest OOB score: 0.789   ← matches test without touching it

Notice the forest also reaches 99.8% on training. That is expected: each tree is still grown to purity. The difference is what happens on unseen rows, and that is the only number a business cares about.

Snippet 3 · How many trees are enough?

python
# Sweep n_estimators; watch test AUC saturate. warm_start=True reuses trees already grown.
rf = RandomForestClassifier(warm_start=True, oob_score=True, n_jobs=-1, random_state=42)
for n in [1, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100, 200, 300, 500]:
    rf.set_params(n_estimators=n)
    rf.fit(X_tr, y_tr)                              # only grows the extra trees
    auc = roc_auc_score(y_te, rf.predict_proba(X_te)[:, 1])
    print(f"{n:4d} trees  test AUC={auc:.3f}")
#   1 trees  test AUC=0.662     10 trees  0.796     100 trees  0.825
#   5 trees  test AUC=0.773     50 trees  0.820     300 trees  0.827   (flat after ~200)

Snippet 4 · Which importance to trust

python
from sklearn.inspection import permutation_importance

imp_gini = pd.Series(rf.feature_importances_, index=X.columns).sort_values(ascending=False)

# shuffle each column of the TEST set 5 times, measure the drop in AUC
pi = permutation_importance(rf, X_te, y_te, scoring="roc_auc", n_repeats=5, random_state=42, n_jobs=-1)
imp_perm = pd.Series(pi.importances_mean, index=X.columns).sort_values(ascending=False)

print(pd.concat([imp_gini.head(8), imp_perm.head(8)], axis=1, keys=["impurity", "permutation"]))
# impurity says gender_Male = 0.029 (rank 7); permutation says gender_Male ≈ 0.000.
# permutation top-3: tenure, Contract_Two year, TotalCharges → these drive churn.

Snippet 5 · The knobs that matter (and the three cousins)

python
# Defaults: n_estimators=100, max_features='sqrt' (classification), max_depth=None, bootstrap=True
rf = RandomForestClassifier(
    n_estimators=300,          # more = smoother, slower; 200–500 is typical
    max_features="sqrt",       # features per split; try 0.3–0.5 of columns for regression
    min_samples_leaf=5,        # stops tiny noisy leaves; the most useful regulariser
    class_weight="balanced",   # churners are 26% → up-weight them (raises recall, AUC 0.843)
    n_jobs=-1, random_state=42, oob_score=True)
rf.fit(X_tr, y_tr)
print(rf.oob_score_, roc_auc_score(y_te, rf.predict_proba(X_te)[:, 1]))
python
# Plain bagging: bootstrap rows, but every split sees ALL features (no de-correlation trick)
from sklearn.ensemble import BaggingClassifier
bag = BaggingClassifier(estimator=DecisionTreeClassifier(),
                        n_estimators=300, n_jobs=-1, random_state=42).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
print(roc_auc_score(y_te, bag.predict_proba(X_te)[:, 1]))   # 0.819 < forest's 0.827
# Bagging works with ANY base model: swap in LogisticRegression, KNN, SVC...
python
# Extra-Trees: random thresholds instead of best thresholds → even lower variance, faster
from sklearn.ensemble import ExtraTreesClassifier
et = ExtraTreesClassifier(n_estimators=300, n_jobs=-1, random_state=42).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
print(roc_auc_score(y_te, et.predict_proba(X_te)[:, 1]))   # 0.801 here; wins on some noisy datasets
python
# Same idea for numbers: each tree predicts a value, the forest returns the mean
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestRegressor
from sklearn.datasets import fetch_california_housing
H = fetch_california_housing()
rfr = RandomForestRegressor(n_estimators=200, max_features=0.5, n_jobs=-1, random_state=42)
# default max_features for regression is 1.0 (all features); 0.3–0.5 usually de-correlates better
Production habit

Random Forest does not need feature scaling, handles mixed numeric/categorical (after encoding), tolerates outliers, and rarely breaks. That is why it is the "first model" at work: train it in two minutes, use its OOB score as a baseline, then decide whether XGBoost (tomorrow) or a neural network is worth the extra effort.

Q: Why does Random Forest overfit less than a single tree?

Each tree is high-variance; averaging B de-correlated trees divides the independent variance by B. Bootstrap rows and random feature subsets keep the correlation ρ low, so the ρσ² floor is small.

Q: What is the OOB score and when would you still use a test set?

Each tree misses ~37% of rows; predicting those rows with only the trees that never saw them gives a free CV-like estimate. You still need a test set for the final report, because you tuned against OOB.

Q: Your RF says customer ID is the most important feature. What happened?

Impurity importance is biased toward high-cardinality columns; an ID has one split point per row. Drop it and use permutation importance on held-out data to check the rest.

07 · Quiz

Three questions, thirty seconds each

3min

You increase n_estimators from 100 to 1000. What happens to test error?

Averaging cannot increase variance; error drops and converges to a limit set by the tree correlation ρ. After ~200–500 trees you only pay in time.

Roughly what fraction of unique training rows does one bootstrap sample contain?

A row is left out with probability (1 − 1/n)ⁿ → 1/e ≈ 0.368, so about 63.2% of unique rows appear; the missing 36.8% are the tree's OOB rows.

What does max_features='sqrt' change compared with plain bagging?

Bagging alone lets every tree grab the same dominant feature at the root. Restricting each split to a random √p features forces diversity, which is the "random" in Random Forest.
